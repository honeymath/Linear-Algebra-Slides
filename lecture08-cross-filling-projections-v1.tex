\section{Cross-Filling a Projection}

\begin{frame}{Where We Are}

From Lecture 7, a \x{projection} $P$ satisfies $P^2 = P$:

\vspace{0.3em}

\begin{center}
\begin{tikzpicture}[scale=0.5]
\draw[ultra thick](-5,0)--(5,0) node[right]{Floor $= \operatorname{Col}(P)$};
\draw[ultra thick,->,blue](0,0)--(3,2);
\node[blue] at (3.4,2) {$\mathbf{v}$};
\draw[ultra thick,->,red](0,0)--(2,0) node[below]{$P\mathbf{v}$};
\draw[dashed,gray](3,2)--(2,0);
\foreach\x in {-2,...,5}{
	\draw [orange,->](\x,4)--(\x-2,0);
}
\draw[fill=red] (0,0) circle[radius=0.1] node[below left]{$O$};
\node[orange] at (4.5,3.5) {\small sunlight};
\end{tikzpicture}
\end{center}

\vspace{0.3em}

From Lecture 3, any matrix decomposes via \x{cross-filling}:
$$A = R_1 + R_2 + \cdots + R_r \quad\text{(rank-1 pieces)}$$

\vspace{0.3em}

\x{Today's question}: What do the pieces look like when we cross-fill a \x{projection}?

\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{The Main Theorem (Preview)}

\begin{alertblock}{Cross-Filling Projections}
When we cross-fill a projection $P = R_1 + R_2 + \cdots + R_r$:

\begin{enumerate}
\item Every rank-1 piece is \x{automatically a projection}: $R_i^2 = R_i$
\item All pieces are \x{mutually annihilating}: $R_i R_j = 0$ for $i \neq j$
\item \x{Rank equals trace}: $\operatorname{rank}(P) = \operatorname{trace}(P)$
\end{enumerate}

None of this happens for general matrices!
\end{alertblock}

\vspace{0.5em}

Let's \x{verify} this with examples, then \x{prove} why it works.

\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Verify: A Rank-2 Projection (Step 1)}

$$P = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & \crs{0} & 0 \\ \crs{-1} & \pvt{1} & \crs{0} \\ -1 & \crs{0} & 1 \end{pmatrix} \quad (P^2 = P \;\checkmark)$$

\x{First pivot} at $(2,2)$, value $= \pvt{1}$:

$$R_1 = \underbrace{\frac{1}{\pvt{1}}\begin{pmatrix} \crs{0} \\ \crs{1} \\ \crs{0} \end{pmatrix}}_{\text{column} \div \text{pivot}} \cdot \underbrace{\begin{pmatrix} \crs{-1} & \crs{1} & \crs{0} \end{pmatrix}}_{\text{row}}
= \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 0 & 0 \\ -1 & 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix}$$

\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Verify: A Rank-2 Projection (Step 2)}

\x{Second pivot} at $(3,3)$, value $= \pvt{1}$ (in the remainder):

$$P - R_1 = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 0 & \crs{0} \\ 0 & 0 & \crs{0} \\ \crs{-1} & \crs{0} & \pvt{1} \end{pmatrix}$$

$$R_2 = \underbrace{\frac{1}{\pvt{1}}\begin{pmatrix} \crs{0} \\ \crs{0} \\ \crs{1} \end{pmatrix}}_{\text{column} \div \text{pivot}} \cdot \underbrace{\begin{pmatrix} \crs{-1} & \crs{0} & \crs{1} \end{pmatrix}}_{\text{row}}
= \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 0 \\ -1 & 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix}$$

\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Verify: Check the Theorem's Predictions}

\x{Cross-filling decomposition}:

$$P = \underbrace{\begin{pmatrix} 0 & 0 & 0 \\ -1 & 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix}}_{R_1} + \underbrace{\begin{pmatrix} 0 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 0 \\ -1 & 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix}}_{R_2}$$

\vspace{0.3em}

$$R_1^2 = R_1 \;\checkmark \qquad R_2^2 = R_2 \;\checkmark \qquad (\text{each piece is a projection})$$

$$R_1 R_2 = 0 \;\checkmark \qquad R_2 R_1 = 0 \;\checkmark \qquad (\text{mutually annihilating})$$

$$\operatorname{trace}(P) = 0 + 1 + 1 = 2 = \operatorname{rank}(P) \;\checkmark$$

\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Verify: Product Form and $VU = I$}

$$P = \underbrace{\begin{pmatrix} | & | \\ \bl{\mathbf{u}_1} & \rd{\mathbf{u}_2} \\ | & | \end{pmatrix}}_{U} \underbrace{\begin{pmatrix} - & \bl{\mathbf{v}_1^T} & - \\ - & \rd{\mathbf{v}_2^T} & - \end{pmatrix}}_{V} = \begin{pmatrix} | & | \\ \bl{0} & \rd{0} \\ \bl{1} & \rd{0} \\ \bl{0} & \rd{1} \\ | & | \end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix} \bl{-1} & \bl{1} & \bl{0} \\ \rd{-1} & \rd{0} & \rd{1} \end{pmatrix}$$

\vspace{0.3em}

$$VU = \begin{pmatrix}
\bl{\mathbf{v}_1^T}\bl{\mathbf{u}_1} & \bl{\mathbf{v}_1^T}\rd{\mathbf{u}_2} \\
\rd{\mathbf{v}_2^T}\bl{\mathbf{u}_1} & \rd{\mathbf{v}_2^T}\rd{\mathbf{u}_2}
\end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} \bl{1} & 0 \\ 0 & \rd{1} \end{pmatrix} = I_2 \quad\checkmark$$

\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{For Contrast: A Non-Projection}

Does this happen for \x{every} matrix? \quad \x{No!}

\vspace{0.3em}

$$A = \begin{pmatrix} \pvt{2} & \crs{4} \\ \crs{1} & 2 \end{pmatrix} \quad (\text{rank } 1, \text{ but } A^2 \neq A)$$

Cross-fill at pivot $(1,1)$, value $= \pvt{2}$:

$$A = \underbrace{\frac{1}{\pvt{2}}\begin{pmatrix} \crs{2} \\ \crs{1} \end{pmatrix}}_{\text{col} \div \text{pivot}} \cdot \underbrace{\begin{pmatrix} \crs{2} & \crs{4} \end{pmatrix}}_{\text{row}} = UV$$

$$VU = \frac{1}{2}\begin{pmatrix} 2 & 4 \end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix} 2 \\ 1 \end{pmatrix} = 4 \neq I_1 \qquad \operatorname{trace}(A) = 4 \neq 1 = \operatorname{rank}(A)$$

The theorem \x{fails} --- because $A$ is not a projection.

\end{frame}

% ═══════════════════════════════════════
% §2 THE PROOF: VU = I
% ═══════════════════════════════════════
\section{Why It Works: $P = UV$ Implies $VU = I$}

\begin{frame}{The Key Theorem}

\begin{thm}[$VU = I$ Automatic]
Let $P$ be a projection ($P^2 = P$). Suppose $P = UV$ where:
\begin{itemize}
\item $U$ is $n \times r$ with linearly independent columns
\item $V$ is $r \times n$ with linearly independent rows
\end{itemize}

Then $VU = I_r$ \x{automatically}.
\end{thm}

\vspace{1em}

This is the \x{proof mechanism} that explains everything we just verified.

\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Proof: Three Lines}

Expand $P^2 = P$ using $P = UV$:

$$\underbrace{UV \cdot UV}_{P^2} = \underbrace{UV}_{P}$$

\vspace{0.3em}

\x{Step 1}: Left-cancel $U$ (columns of $U$ are linearly independent):

$$UVUV = UV \quad\Longrightarrow\quad VUV = V$$

\vspace{0.3em}

\x{Step 2}: Right-cancel $V$ (rows of $V$ are linearly independent):

$$VUV = V \quad\Longrightarrow\quad VU = I_r$$

\vspace{0.3em}

Done! The proof uses \x{only} $P^2 = P$ and cancellation. \qed

\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Now We Can Explain Everything}

Write $P = UV$ with $VU = I_r$:

$$U = \begin{pmatrix} | & | & & | \\ \bl{\mathbf{u}_1} & \rd{\mathbf{u}_2} & \cdots & \mathbf{u}_r \\ | & | & & | \end{pmatrix}, \qquad V = \begin{pmatrix} - & \bl{\mathbf{v}_1^T} & - \\ - & \rd{\mathbf{v}_2^T} & - \\ & \vdots & \\ - & \mathbf{v}_r^T & - \end{pmatrix}$$

Each rank-1 piece: $R_i = \mathbf{u}_i \mathbf{v}_i^T$

\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{What Does $VU = I_r$ Look Like?}

$$VU = \begin{pmatrix}
\bl{\mathbf{v}_1^T}\bl{\mathbf{u}_1} & \bl{\mathbf{v}_1^T}\rd{\mathbf{u}_2} & \cdots & \bl{\mathbf{v}_1^T}\mathbf{u}_r \\
\rd{\mathbf{v}_2^T}\bl{\mathbf{u}_1} & \rd{\mathbf{v}_2^T}\rd{\mathbf{u}_2} & \cdots & \rd{\mathbf{v}_2^T}\mathbf{u}_r \\
\vdots & \vdots & \ddots & \vdots \\
\mathbf{v}_r^T\bl{\mathbf{u}_1} & \mathbf{v}_r^T\rd{\mathbf{u}_2} & \cdots & \mathbf{v}_r^T\mathbf{u}_r
\end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix}
\bl{1} & 0 & \cdots & 0 \\
0 & \rd{1} & \cdots & 0 \\
\vdots & \vdots & \ddots & \vdots \\
0 & 0 & \cdots & 1
\end{pmatrix} = I_r$$

\vspace{0.3em}

\x{Diagonal}: $\mathbf{v}_i^T \mathbf{u}_i = 1$ \quad (same-color pairs multiply to 1)

\x{Off-diagonal}: $\mathbf{v}_i^T \mathbf{u}_j = 0$ for $i \neq j$ \quad (different-color pairs multiply to 0)

\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Each Piece Is a Projection}

\x{Goal}: Show $R_i^2 = R_i$ using $\bl{\mathbf{v}_i^T} \bl{\mathbf{u}_i} = 1$.

\vspace{0.5em}

$$R_i^2 = (\bl{\mathbf{u}_i} \bl{\mathbf{v}_i^T})(\bl{\mathbf{u}_i} \bl{\mathbf{v}_i^T}) = \bl{\mathbf{u}_i} \underbrace{(\bl{\mathbf{v}_i^T} \bl{\mathbf{u}_i})}_{= 1} \bl{\mathbf{v}_i^T} = \bl{\mathbf{u}_i} \bl{\mathbf{v}_i^T} = R_i \quad\checkmark$$

\vspace{0.5em}

Each cross-filling piece $R_i = \mathbf{u}_i \mathbf{v}_i^T$ is \x{automatically a rank-1 projection}!

\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Mutually Annihilating Pieces}

\x{Goal}: Show $R_i R_j = 0$ (for $i \neq j$) using $\bl{\mathbf{v}_i^T} \rd{\mathbf{u}_j} = 0$.

\vspace{0.5em}

$$R_i R_j = (\bl{\mathbf{u}_i} \bl{\mathbf{v}_i^T})(\rd{\mathbf{u}_j} \rd{\mathbf{v}_j^T}) = \bl{\mathbf{u}_i} \underbrace{(\bl{\mathbf{v}_i^T} \rd{\mathbf{u}_j})}_{= 0} \rd{\mathbf{v}_j^T} = 0 \quad\checkmark$$

\vspace{1em}

\x{Summary}: For a projection $P = R_1 + \cdots + R_r$ (cross-filling decomposition):
\begin{itemize}
\item Each $R_i$ is a rank-1 projection ($R_i^2 = R_i$)
\item Different pieces annihilate ($R_i R_j = 0$ for $i \neq j$)
\end{itemize}

Both follow from the single identity $VU = I_r$.

\end{frame}

% §3 RANK = TRACE
% ═══════════════════════════════════════
\section{Rank Equals Trace for Projections}

\begin{frame}{The Theorem}

\begin{thm}[Rank = Trace for Projections]
For any projection $P$ ($P^2 = P$):
$$\operatorname{rank}(P) = \operatorname{trace}(P)$$
\end{thm}

\textbf{Proof}:

Write $P = UV$ where $\operatorname{rank}(P) = r$. By the previous theorem, $VU = I_r$.

Using the cyclic property of trace:

$$\operatorname{trace}(P) = \operatorname{trace}(UV) = \operatorname{trace}(VU) = \operatorname{trace}(I_r) = r = \operatorname{rank}(P)$$

Done! \qed

\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Two Quick Facts}

\begin{prop}[Rank 0 $\Rightarrow$ Zero Matrix]
A rank $0$ matrix is the zero matrix.
\end{prop}

A zero-dimensional column space means every column is zero. $\quad\checkmark$

\vspace{0.5em}

\begin{prop}[Full-Rank Projection $= I$]
An $n \times n$ projection $P$ with $\operatorname{trace}(P) = n$ must be $P = I_n$.
\end{prop}

\textbf{Proof}: $I_n - P$ is a projection with $\operatorname{trace}(I_n - P) = n - n = 0$.

By rank $=$ trace: $\operatorname{rank}(I_n - P) = 0$, so $I_n - P = 0$. \qed

\vspace{0.5em}

(We will use both of these in the consequences below.)

\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Why Only for Projections?}

The identity $\operatorname{rank} = \operatorname{trace}$ is special to projections.

\vspace{0.3em}

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{l|c|c|c}
& $\operatorname{rank}$ & $\operatorname{trace}$ & Equal? \\
\hline
$\begin{pmatrix} 2 & 4 \\ 1 & 2 \end{pmatrix}$ (not projection) & 1 & 4 & \texttimes \\[0.5em]
$\begin{pmatrix} 3 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix}$ (not projection) & 1 & 3 & \texttimes \\[0.5em]
$\frac{1}{5}\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ 2 & 4 \end{pmatrix}$ (projection) & 1 & 1 & \checkmark \\[0.5em]
$\begin{pmatrix} 0 & 0 & 0 \\ -1 & 1 & 0 \\ -1 & 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix}$ (projection) & 2 & 2 & \checkmark \\
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\vspace{0.3em}

$\operatorname{rank} = \operatorname{trace}$ is a \x{fingerprint} of projection matrices.

\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Quick Application: Read the Rank from the Diagonal}

\begin{exampleblock}{Exercise}
The following matrix is a projection ($P^2 = P$). What is its rank?

$$P = \begin{pmatrix} \bl{0} & 0 & 0 & 0 & 0 \\ -1 & \bl{1} & 0 & 0 & 0 \\ -1 & 0 & \bl{1} & 0 & 0 \\ -1 & 0 & 0 & \bl{1} & 0 \\ -1 & 0 & 0 & 0 & \bl{1} \end{pmatrix}$$
\end{exampleblock}

\vspace{0.5em}

\x{Answer}: $\operatorname{rank}(P) = \operatorname{trace}(P) = 0 + 1 + 1 + 1 + 1 = 4$.

\vspace{0.5em}

No cross-filling needed --- just read the diagonal!

\end{frame}

\section{Diagonal Cross-Filling: The Symmetric Case}

\begin{frame}{What If $P$ Is Also Symmetric?}

We proved: cross-filling \x{any} projection gives rank-1 projections.

\vspace{0.5em}

Now suppose $P$ is an \x{orthogonal projection}: $P^2 = P$ and $P^T = P$.

\vspace{0.5em}

If we choose pivots \x{on the diagonal}, something extra happens:

\vspace{0.3em}

\begin{itemize}
\item The pivot row and pivot column are \x{transposes of each other} (by symmetry)
\item So each rank-1 piece has the form $R_i = \mathbf{v}_i \mathbf{v}_i^T$ (symmetric!)
\item Each piece is an \x{orthogonal} rank-1 projection
\end{itemize}

\vspace{0.5em}

Cross-filling a symmetric matrix \x{along the diagonal} preserves symmetry.

\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{The Theorem}

\begin{thm}[Diagonal Cross-Filling of Orthogonal Projections]
Let $P$ be an orthogonal projection ($P^2 = P$, $P^T = P$) of rank $r$.

Diagonal cross-filling decomposes $P$ into:
$$P = \mathbf{v}_1\mathbf{v}_1^T + \mathbf{v}_2\mathbf{v}_2^T + \cdots + \mathbf{v}_r\mathbf{v}_r^T$$
where $\mathbf{v}_1, \ldots, \mathbf{v}_r$ is an \x{orthonormal basis} of $\operatorname{Col}(P)$.
\end{thm}

\vspace{0.5em}

\x{Why orthonormal?}

Since $VU = I_r$, and diagonal cross-filling makes $V = U^T$:
$$U^T U = I_r$$

The columns of $U$ are the $\mathbf{v}_i$, and $U^T U = I_r$ means they are orthonormal.

\end{frame}

\begin{frame}[fragile]{Example: Diagonal Cross-Filling (Step 1)}

$$P = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & \frac{1}{2} & \frac{1}{2} \\ 0 & \frac{1}{2} & \frac{1}{2} \end{pmatrix} \quad (P^2 = P,\; P^T = P \;\checkmark)$$

\x{Pivot $(\pvt{1},\pvt{1})$}, value $= \pvt{1}$:

$$R_1 = \underbrace{\frac{1}{\pvt{1}}\begin{pmatrix} \crs{1} \\ \crs{0} \\ \crs{0} \end{pmatrix}}_{\text{column} \div \text{pivot}} \cdot \underbrace{\begin{pmatrix} \crs{1} & \crs{0} & \crs{0} \end{pmatrix}}_{\text{row} = \text{column}^T!} = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix} \quad\text{(symmetric }\checkmark\text{)}$$

Key: column and row are \x{transposes} of each other (by $P^T = P$).

\end{frame}

\begin{frame}[fragile]{Example: Diagonal Cross-Filling (Step 2)}

Remainder: $\begin{pmatrix} 0 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & \frac{1}{2} & \frac{1}{2} \\ 0 & \frac{1}{2} & \frac{1}{2} \end{pmatrix}$. \; \x{Pivot $(\pvt{2},\pvt{2})$}, value $= \pvt{\frac{1}{2}}$:

$$R_2 = \underbrace{\frac{1}{\pvt{\frac{1}{2}}}\begin{pmatrix} \crs{0} \\ \crs{\frac{1}{2}} \\ \crs{\frac{1}{2}} \end{pmatrix}}_{\text{column} \div \text{pivot}} \cdot \underbrace{\begin{pmatrix} \crs{0} & \crs{\frac{1}{2}} & \crs{\frac{1}{2}} \end{pmatrix}}_{\text{row} = \text{column}^T!} = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & \frac{1}{2} & \frac{1}{2} \\ 0 & \frac{1}{2} & \frac{1}{2} \end{pmatrix} \quad\text{(symmetric }\checkmark\text{)}$$

\vspace{0.5em}

$$P = \underbrace{\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix}}_{R_1 = \mathbf{v}_1\mathbf{v}_1^T} + \underbrace{\begin{pmatrix} 0 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & \frac{1}{2} & \frac{1}{2} \\ 0 & \frac{1}{2} & \frac{1}{2} \end{pmatrix}}_{R_2 = \mathbf{v}_2\mathbf{v}_2^T}$$

\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Example: Orthonormal Basis}

Normalizing the column vectors:

$$\mathbf{v}_1 = \begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \\ 0 \end{pmatrix}, \qquad \mathbf{v}_2 = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\begin{pmatrix} 0 \\ 1 \\ 1 \end{pmatrix}$$

\vspace{0.5em}

\x{Check orthonormality}:
$$\mathbf{v}_1^T\mathbf{v}_1 = 1 \;\checkmark \qquad \mathbf{v}_2^T\mathbf{v}_2 = 1 \;\checkmark \qquad \mathbf{v}_1^T\mathbf{v}_2 = 0 \;\checkmark$$

\vspace{0.5em}

\x{Check decomposition}:
$$P = \mathbf{v}_1\mathbf{v}_1^T + \mathbf{v}_2\mathbf{v}_2^T \;\checkmark$$

\vspace{0.5em}

Diagonal cross-filling gave us an \x{orthonormal basis} of $\operatorname{Col}(P)$ for free!

\end{frame}

\section{Three Consequences}

\begin{frame}{Rank-1 Projections}

\begin{defi}[Rank-1 Projection]
A \x{rank-1 projection} is a matrix $P$ with $P^2 = P$ and $\operatorname{rank}(P) = 1$.
\end{defi}

\vspace{0.3em}

Geometrically: the floor is a \x{line} (1-dimensional).

\begin{center}
\begin{tikzpicture}[scale=0.5]
% Line (floor) - 1D
\draw[ultra thick, blue](-4,0)--(4,0) node[right]{Floor (a line)};
% A vector projected
\draw[ultra thick,->,blue](0,0)--(2,2);
\node[blue] at (2.4,2) {$\mathbf{v}$};
\draw[ultra thick,->,red](0,0)--(1,0) node[below]{$P\mathbf{v}$};
\draw[dashed,gray](2,2)--(1,0);
% Sunlight: direction (-1.5,-3) proportional to (-1,-2), same as v-Pv
\foreach\x in {-2,...,3}{
	\draw [orange,->](\x,3)--(\x-1.5,0);
}
\draw[fill=red] (0,0) circle[radius=0.08] node[below left]{$O$};
\node[orange] at (3,2.5) {\small sunlight};
\end{tikzpicture}
\end{center}

\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Rank-1 Projection: Example}

\x{Example in $\mathbb{R}^3$}: Project onto the line through $\begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ 2 \\ 3 \end{pmatrix}$:

$$P = \frac{1}{14}\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 2 & 3 \\ 2 & 4 & 6 \\ 3 & 6 & 9 \end{pmatrix} \quad \operatorname{rank} = 1, \; \operatorname{trace} = \frac{1+4+9}{14} = 1$$

\vspace{0.5em}

$P = \mathbf{u}\mathbf{v}^T$ with $\mathbf{v}^T\mathbf{u} = 1$: this is \x{automatically} a projection.

\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Consequence 1: Rank-1 Projection Characterization}

\begin{cor}[Rank-1 Projection Characterization]
For a square matrix $A$, consider three conditions:
\begin{enumerate}
\item $\operatorname{rank}(A) = 1$
\item $\operatorname{trace}(A) = 1$
\item $A^2 = A$ \quad (projection)
\end{enumerate}

\x{Any two imply the third.}
\end{cor}

\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Proof of Rank-1 Characterization}

\x{(1) + (2) $\Rightarrow$ (3)}: rank $= 1$ and trace $= 1$.

Write $A = \mathbf{u}\mathbf{v}^T$. Then $\operatorname{trace}(A) = \mathbf{v}^T\mathbf{u} = 1$.
$$A^2 = \mathbf{u}\underbrace{(\mathbf{v}^T\mathbf{u})}_{=1}\mathbf{v}^T = \mathbf{u}\mathbf{v}^T = A \quad\checkmark$$

\vspace{0.5em}

\x{(1) + (3) $\Rightarrow$ (2)}: rank $= 1$ and $A^2 = A$.

Write $A = UV$ ($1 \times 1$ middle). By $VU = I$: $VU = 1$.
$$\operatorname{trace}(A) = \operatorname{trace}(UV) = \operatorname{trace}(VU) = 1 \quad\checkmark$$

\vspace{0.5em}

\x{(2) + (3) $\Rightarrow$ (1)}: trace $= 1$ and $A^2 = A$.

By rank $=$ trace: $\operatorname{rank}(A) = \operatorname{trace}(A) = 1$. $\quad\checkmark$

\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Why Is This Useful?}

To check whether a rank-1 matrix $A = \mathbf{u}\mathbf{v}^T$ is a projection:

\vspace{0.5em}

\begin{center}
Just compute $\operatorname{trace}(A) = \mathbf{v}^T\mathbf{u}$.

\vspace{0.3em}

If $\mathbf{v}^T\mathbf{u} = 1$, then $A^2 = A$ is \x{automatic}.
\end{center}

\vspace{1em}

No need to multiply the full matrix!

\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Practice: Is It a Rank-1 Projection?}

Use the characterization: rank $= 1$ and trace $= 1$ $\Rightarrow$ projection.

\vspace{0.3em}

\x{1.} $A = \frac{1}{14}\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 2 & 3 \\ 2 & 4 & 6 \\ 3 & 6 & 9 \end{pmatrix}$
\quad rank $= 1$, \; trace $= \frac{1+4+9}{14} = 1$ \quad $\Rightarrow$ \x{Yes} $\checkmark$

\vspace{0.3em}

\x{2.} $B = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & -1 & 0 \\ -1 & 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix}$
\quad rank $= 1$, \; trace $= 1 + 1 + 0 = 2 \neq 1$ \quad $\Rightarrow$ \x{No} $\times$

\vspace{0.3em}

\x{3.} $C = \frac{1}{2}\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 & 1 \\ 0 & 0 & 0 \\ 1 & 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix}$
\quad rank $= 1$, \; trace $= \frac{1}{2} + 0 + \frac{1}{2} = 1$ \quad $\Rightarrow$ \x{Yes} $\checkmark$

\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Consequence 2: $UV = I$ Forces Square}

\begin{cor}[$UV = I$ Forces Square Matrices]
Suppose $U$ is $n \times r$ and $V$ is $r \times n$ with $UV = I_n$ and $r \leq n$.

Then $r = n$, and both $U, V$ are square invertible with $VU = UV = I_n$.
\end{cor}

\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Proof: $UV = I$ Forces Square}

\x{Step 1}: $VU$ is a projection.
$$(VU)(VU) = V\underbrace{(UV)}_{= I_n}U = VU \quad\checkmark$$

\x{Step 2}: $I_r - VU$ is also a projection (by Lecture 7).

\x{Step 3}: Compute the trace of $I_r - VU$.
$$\operatorname{trace}(I_r - VU) = r - \operatorname{trace}(VU) = r - \operatorname{trace}(UV) = r - \operatorname{trace}(I_n) = r - n$$

\x{Step 4}: Apply rank $=$ trace.

Since $I_r - VU$ is a projection: $\operatorname{rank}(I_r - VU) = r - n$.

Since $\operatorname{rank} \geq 0$: $r \geq n$. Combined with $r \leq n$: \x{$r = n$}.

\vspace{0.3em}

\x{Step 5}: $\operatorname{rank}(I_r - VU) = 0$, so $I_r - VU = 0$ (rank $0$ = zero matrix), giving $VU = I_n$. \qed

\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Geometric Meaning}

You \x{cannot} have a ``thin'' matrix times a ``fat'' matrix equal the identity:

\vspace{0.5em}

\begin{center}
\begin{tikzpicture}[scale=0.5]
% U: tall thin
\draw[fill=blue!20] (0,0) rectangle (1,3);
\node at (0.5,1.5) {$U$};
\node[below] at (0.5,-0.3) {\small $n \times r$};
% V: short fat
\draw[fill=red!20] (2,0.5) rectangle (5,1.5);

\node[below] at (3.5,-0.3) {\small $r \times n$};
% =
\node at (6,1) {$=$};

\end{tikzpicture}
\end{center}

\vspace{0.5em}

If $UV = I$, both factors \x{must be square and invertible}.

\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Important Special Case}

\begin{prop}[One-Sided Inverse $\Rightarrow$ Two-Sided]
If $F$ and $V$ are both $n \times n$ and $FV = I_n$, then $VF = I_n$.
\end{prop}

\textbf{Proof}: Apply the previous corollary with $r = n$. \qed

\vspace{1em}

\x{Recall Lecture 1}: We defined ``invertible'' by assuming \x{both} $AB = I$ and $BA = I$.

\vspace{0.5em}

Now we know: for square matrices, \x{one equation suffices}!

$$FV = I_n \quad\Longrightarrow\quad VF = I_n$$

\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Consequence 3: Full Rank $\iff$ Invertible}

\begin{thm}[Full Rank $\iff$ Invertible]
An $n \times n$ matrix $X$ is invertible if and only if $\operatorname{rank}(X) = n$.
\end{thm}

\vspace{0.5em}

\x{($\Rightarrow$) Invertible $\Rightarrow$ full rank}:

Suppose $XY = I_n$. Cross-fill $X = UV$ with $\operatorname{rank}(X) = k \leq n$.

Then $U(VY) = I_n$, where $U$ is $n \times k$ and $VY$ is $k \times n$.

By ``$UV = I$ forces square'': $k = n$. So $\operatorname{rank}(X) = n$. $\checkmark$

\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Full Rank $\Rightarrow$ Invertible (Part 1: $U$ is invertible)}

\x{($\Leftarrow$) Full rank $\Rightarrow$ invertible}:

Suppose $\operatorname{rank}(X) = n$. Cross-fill $X = UV$ (both $n \times n$).

\vspace{0.5em}

\x{Step 1}: Cross-fill the augmented matrix $(X \mid I_n)$ with the \x{same pivots}:

$$(X \mid I_n) = U(V \mid W)$$

Comparing: $X = UV$ and $I_n = UW$.

\vspace{0.5em}

\x{Step 2}: $UW = I_n$ (both $n \times n$), so by Proposition: $WU = I_n$.

Therefore $U$ is invertible with $U^{-1} = W$.

\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Full Rank $\Rightarrow$ Invertible (Part 2: $V$ is invertible)}

\x{Step 3}: Cross-fill the stacked matrix with the same pivots:

$$\begin{pmatrix} X \\ I_n \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} U \\ Z \end{pmatrix} V$$

Comparing: $X = UV$ and $I_n = ZV$.

\vspace{0.5em}

$ZV = I_n$ (both $n \times n$), so by Proposition: $VZ = I_n$.

Therefore $V$ is invertible with $V^{-1} = Z$.

\vspace{0.5em}

\x{Step 4}: $X = UV$ with both invertible, so $X^{-1} = V^{-1}U^{-1}$. \qed

\end{frame}

\section{Summary}

\begin{frame}{What We Proved Today}

\x{1.\ Cross-filling projections}: Each rank-1 piece is itself a projection, and they are mutually annihilating.

\vspace{0.2em}

\x{2.\ $VU = I$ Automatic}: If $P = UV$ with $P^2 = P$ and full-rank factors, then $VU = I$.

\vspace{0.2em}

\x{3.\ Rank $=$ Trace}: For any projection $P$: $\operatorname{rank}(P) = \operatorname{trace}(P)$.

\vspace{0.2em}

\x{4.\ Diagonal cross-filling}: For orthogonal projections, produces orthonormal basis.

\vspace{0.2em}

\x{5.\ Rank-1 Characterization}: Any two of $\{\text{rank}=1, \text{trace}=1, A^2=A\}$ imply the third.

\vspace{0.2em}

\x{6.\ $UV = I$ Forces Square}: Both factors must be $n \times n$ and invertible.

\vspace{0.2em}

\x{7.\ Full Rank $\iff$ Invertible}: An $n \times n$ matrix is invertible iff rank $= n$.

\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Preview: Lecture 9}

\x{Lecture 9}: Compatible Projections

\vspace{1em}

Today we proved that \x{cross-filling} a projection produces mutually annihilating rank-1 projections.

\vspace{0.5em}

\x{Does this generalize?}

\vspace{0.5em}

Suppose we write $P = R_1 + \cdots + R_k$ (not necessarily from cross-filling), with $\operatorname{rank}(R_1) + \cdots + \operatorname{rank}(R_k) \leq \operatorname{rank}(P)$.

\vspace{0.5em}

Must each $R_i$ still be a projection? Must $R_i R_j = 0$?

\vspace{1em}

The answer is \x{yes} --- this is the \x{projection decomposition theorem}.

\end{frame}
